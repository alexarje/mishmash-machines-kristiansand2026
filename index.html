MishMash Opening Conference · Machines session

Building a Norwegian Ecosystem for AI and Creativity

Kilden, Kristiansand · 14 September 2026

Ten minutes of map, then the panel. This is not a recommendation and there is no common solution: the point is to lay out the options, find the holes, and see what could improve. Everyone here has used an AI system this week; most of us used one we do not own and cannot inspect.

Three questions I get in my inbox, and they are the whole session in miniature. About a minute and a half. Each question on a click, and its general form on the next. One: how do I get on Sigma2 to train a model. Short answer: through your institution, in one of two application rounds a year, or by being added to a project by someone who already has one; if you are a freelance artist or a museum, there is no route at all. Click: that is the first general question, which tools can we use. Two: can I get funding for Claude Code. Short answer: a top-tier seat is about 2 500 kroner a month, 200 of them is 6 million a year, and nobody has budgeted it; the sustainable version is pay per use through a gateway, and the honest answer is that for most jobs a smaller model is good enough. Click: which tools should we use, and which should we not. Three: an avatar of yourself from Bergen to Trondheim in 10 milliseconds. Bergen to Trondheim is about 430 kilometres in a straight line, and light in a fibre needs about 2 milliseconds each way before a single router or a single rendered frame; the rest of the budget goes to the model, and nothing on the map today does that for you. Click: which tools do we need to develop. Those three general questions structure the whole session. And the map is not only for MishMash researchers: universities and university colleges, research institutes, students from bachelor to PhD, teachers and schools, freelance artists, the public sector with museums, libraries, broadcasters and municipalities, and the private sector with studios, film, games and startups.

Rent or run

The first fork in the road, and the one everyone in the room has already taken this week: rent a commercial service, or run open models on hardware you control. And a middle road most people forget.
Rent. The best models today, zero setup, any device. But no weights, no reproducibility, data leave the room, terms change, and it is the same model for everyone. Click: run it yourself. Full control, private, trainable on your own material. One step behind the frontier, you carry the setup and the energy bill. Click: the middle road. Open models on somebody else's GPUs: Hugging Face, Mistral's own platform, EU-hosted providers, or an institution-hosted chat service such as UiO GPT. Open weights, rented hardware. A minute and a half. Depth below: the six questions to ask of any tool, then the frontends, the models and the licences, for anyone who asks what exactly they can rent or run.

Six questions to ask of any tool

Which model?

Name and version. "AI" is not an answer.

Where does it run?

Your machine, your institution, an EU data centre, the US.

Where do my data go?

Prompt, files, recordings. Stored? Trained on?

Who owns the output?

And can you sell a work made with it?

Same result next year?

Reproducibility for research; continuity for a repertoire piece.

What does it cost?

Per month, per hour of GPU, per kilowatt-hour.

Depth slide. The six questions apply to every option in the rest of the map. For a researcher the fifth is reproducibility. For a musician it is whether the piece can be performed again in five years.
Depth. Four families, one per click. Chat: the web apps, an institution-hosted service like UiO GPT, GPT NTNU or Copilot under UiB's Microsoft agreement, or Open WebUI and LM Studio on top of a local model; this is where teachers and most artists start. Code and agents: VS Code with Copilot or Continue, Claude Code, Cursor, Jupyter, Educloud On Demand on Fox. Creative tools: Max with nn~ and FluCoMa, Pure Data, Ableton with Neutone, TouchDesigner, ComfyUI for image and video, Unity and Unreal, p5.js with ml5. Programmatic: Python with PyTorch, transformers and diffusers, llama.cpp, MLX, Ollama or vLLM as a local server. The same model can sit behind all four.
Depth. The trick that makes switching cheap: almost everything speaks the same OpenAI-compatible API. One backend per click, from the laptop up to the commercial APIs. Ollama and LM Studio on the laptop, vLLM on a lab server or behind a tunnel to Fox, Mistral and the EU providers, and the commercial APIs. Change one line, keep the workflow. Prototype against the laptop, point at a server for the show.

Tool is not model

Depth. The distinction people most often miss. Top row: tools, the thing you sit in. Click: the models underneath. ChatGPT and UiO GPT are both GPT. VS Code can talk to GPT, Claude or a local Llama. Max with nn~ runs RAVE, and can call a language model too. ComfyUI drives FLUX or Stable Diffusion. Ollama serves Llama or Mistral. Same model in many tools, same tool with many models. When someone says "we use AI", ask which model, where it runs, and where the data go.

Conditions

Community licences and terms of use

  • Llama: "Built with Llama", 700M-user cap, derivatives named Llama
  • Gemma: prohibited-use policy passed on
  • Stability community licence (SD3.5, Stable Audio Open): free under $1M revenue
  • CreativeML OpenRAIL-M (SD 1.5, SDXL): use restrictions travel with the weights
  • NorwAI NorLLM: Nordic organisations

Non-commercial

Research only, CC-BY-NC

  • FLUX.1 dev: non-commercial licence for the model
  • MusicGen: code MIT, weights CC-BY-NC 4.0
  • many research checkpoints on Hugging Face
  • fine for a paper; a commissioned work is commercial
Depth. Three colours with concrete names. Green: permissive, do what you like, keep the notice. Amber: community licences and terms of use; usually fine for artists and researchers, but with strings: attribution, revenue caps, naming rules, use restrictions that travel with the weights. Red: research-only or non-commercial; fine for a paper, a problem for a commissioned work. Two traps: the code and the weights can have different licences, as with MusicGen, and licences change between versions, so read the card of the exact checkpoint. Licences: RAVE MIT; MusicGen weights CC-BY-NC; Stable Audio Open and SD3.5 Stability community licence with a one-million-dollar revenue cap; FLUX.1 schnell Apache 2.0 but FLUX.1 dev non-commercial; Llama community licence; Gemma terms of use; Qwen3 Apache 2.0. Rule of thumb, not legal advice.

Where the compute is

Where the machines are, from the laptop in the room to a hall in Kajaani. Each step up gives more compute and costs more waiting and paperwork.
A minute. Your own hardware first, the layer artists, teachers and students can most realistically own. Left to right: Arduino and ESP32 for keyword spotting and gesture classes, milliseconds, no language model. Raspberry Pi and Jetson for small vision and audio models, an embodied agent in a box. Phone and tablet: on-device models of one to three billion parameters, camera and microphone, always with you, closed platforms. Laptop: seven to fourteen billion chat, Whisper, RAVE on stage. Laptop with a discrete GPU: diffusion, fine-tuning small models. Workstation: seventy billion locally, training small models, serving a studio. Click: memory. Click: which models fit where. The one number that matters is memory: it decides which model fits at all. Compute only decides how fast.
A minute. When your own hardware is not enough: the stair from the room up to Kajaani. Shared server: a GPU box in the lab, ssh, first come first served. VDI: a virtual desktop for licensed software and sensitive data, rarely a real GPU. Institutional HPC: UiO's Fox, NTNU's IDUN, UiA's DGX servers at CAIR, local clusters at UiB and UiT. Slurm, batch jobs, free for the institution's own people, students through a supervisor, collaborators added by a project leader. Most other institutions have their own small servers or nothing. National: Sigma2 and NRIS. Olivia, 448 Grace Hopper chips after the February expansion, plus Saga; application rounds twice a year. KI-fabrikken, opened last year, is the first national channel that explicitly includes business and public sector. European: LUMI in Finland, where Norway is a member, and the EuroHPC AI Factories. Click: what changes as you go up. More compute, more people can share it, data can live next to it. But more waiting, more paperwork, less interactivity. Batch scheduling is the wrong shape for a rehearsal. Depth below: your own hardware rung by rung, how you actually get on, the institutions side by side, and why serving a model and training one need different setups.
Depth. Rule of thumb for language models: parameters times bytes per parameter. A quantised 7 or 8B model is about five gigabytes and runs on any recent laptop. Thirty billion needs about twenty. Seventy needs forty: a machine with a lot of unified memory or two big GPUs. Full precision seventy billion is a server job. Real-time audio models are tiny by comparison.

Inference is not training

Serving a model

  • always on, answers in milliseconds
  • a web endpoint that a class, a stage or a museum connects to
  • a modest GPU, or many of them, or a laptop
  • needs networking, logins and a security review

Training and fine-tuning

  • bursts: hours to weeks, then nothing
  • no endpoint, no users, a queue
  • the biggest GPUs and the fastest storage
  • needs scheduling, checkpoints, data next to compute
Depth. Two loads, two setups, not just two sizes. National HPC is built for the right column: batch jobs in a queue, no open ports, nothing that runs for months. Serving a model, the left column, is what a class, a stage, a museum or a startup actually needs, and it is the thing a cluster will not let you do. Most of the users on the map need the left column, and the only places that offer it are commercial clouds and your own machine.

Workflows do not travel

Laptop to Fox

Different modules, versions, paths. Days of setup.

Fox to Olivia

Same scheduler, different drivers, containers and rules.

Olivia to LUMI

NVIDIA to AMD, CUDA to ROCm. Often a rewrite.

What helps

Containers and pinned environments. Teach it at student level. AI assistants make porting cheap.

Depth. A workflow built on one system is locked to it, and often to one vendor. Something that runs on Fox may not run on Olivia, and Olivia to LUMI is a different GPU vendor altogether. This is a skills problem as much as a hardware problem: it has to be taught at student level, with containers and reproducible environments, and coding assistants now make the porting itself much cheaper than it was. A national ecosystem that ignores this buys hardware that half its users cannot move to.

Who needs what

Now the people. Seven work packages, from a musician on stage to the National Library, and they do not need the same machine. That is why there is no common solution.
Two axes: how much compute, and how sensitive the data. Click. WP1, a musician on stage: milliseconds, a laptop in the room, own recordings; bottom left. WP2, a filmmaker: video generation, hours on a GPU, and the rights of the training data are the issue. WP3, a music therapist: small models but health data, so TSD or fully offline; top left. WP4, a teacher: no GPU at all, a managed tablet or laptop, pupils' data, and a need for free Norwegian tools; far left, high up. Creative AI is not "traditional" AI

AI for a task

  • one task, one right answer
  • batch: throughput matters, latency does not
  • large, clean, labelled data
  • measured by accuracy on a benchmark
  • runs in a data centre, out of sight

AI for creative work

  • open-ended: many good answers, or none
  • real-time and interactive: latency is the constraint
  • small, personal, messy data, often your own
  • measured by taste, context and who is asking
  • on stage, in the room, on the body: control matters
Depth. One distinction that runs through the whole map, and belongs with use-or-develop. Click: the left column. Click: the right. Most of the national AI infrastructure is built for the left column: one well-defined task, big data, batch jobs, accuracy as the measure. Creative work sits in the right column: it is interactive, it needs low latency, the data are small and personal, and whether the result is good is a question of taste and context rather than a benchmark. One caveat: every generative model has some degree of creativity in it, whether or not it is used for a task anyone would call creative. The point is not that the models are different. The point is that the needs are: latency, control, small data and evaluation. The panel comes back to this.

Counted in downloadable objects, Norway looks open: Wikidata, KulturNav, half of DigitaltMuseum, Nasjonalmuseet, the public registers, and Språkbanken, which makes speech the only well-served creative data type. Measured in hours and pages, Norway is closed: the National Library's largest holdings are licensed for reading on site or from a Norwegian address, not for mining, and the broadcasters and news archives are all rights reserved. The reason is not the licences but the law: Norway still has no text-and-data-mining exception. Sweden and Finland have had one since 2023. The bill is in the Storting's family and culture committee now, with written input due 28 September and a report due 3 November. The one exception is the newspaper agreement, which lets the library alone train on newspapers for 45 million kroner a year. And for the creative data types this centre cares most about, there is no open Norwegian dataset at all. Compute without data is a machine with nothing to learn from.

The arithmetic behind the whole strip. A top-tier seat on a commercial coding agent, Claude Max as the example, costs about 2 500 kroner a month. MishMash has more than 200 researchers. If every one of them had a seat, the centre would spend 6 million kroner a year on one product from one vendor, before a single image, video or music service is counted. No institution and no centre has budgeted for that, and it is the wrong shape anyway: a seat costs the same for the person who uses it all day and the person who tried it twice. Oddmund Møgedal from UiO IT put it plainly to the university this week: tokens will be a limited resource for the foreseeable future, there will never be unlimited access to the best models, and the money for the commercial ones has to come from somewhere, so administrations will start counting. National: quotas through one door

  • the gateways and clusters of the big institutions, opened to everyone else on the map
  • a quota per person, paid per use by the unit, across vendors
  • frontier models when they are needed, open models for the rest
  • quota categories for artists, schools and museums, not only researchers

Local: small and smart

  • small open models on the laptop, the board, the phone

  • energy and cost in the room, where you can see them
  • for the stage, the classroom, the museum
One minute, and the frame for the panel. Two extremes, and the answer is somewhere between them, but the extremes are what to argue about. The national road builds on what the big institutions already have, the gateways and the clusters, and opens them to everyone on the map through quotas: a quota per person, a unit that pays for actual use, the frontier models when they are needed and open models for the rest. The missing piece is the categories: nobody has defined an artist, a school or a museum as a user with a quota. The local road is the opposite bet. Small open models on hardware in the room, several small agents doing one job each rather than one large model doing everything, and energy and cost visible on the desk. This is Stefano's world, and it is the direction where a centre can contribute methods rather than money. Short term, only the national road scales to 200 researchers by next year. Long term, only the local road makes the centre independent of any vendor. Removed from the slide, to say from the floor if it fits: the elephant. I would argue the consumption that is not sustainable is not researchers running agents. It is generated feeds, synthetic images and video for social media, the mass of output nobody asked for. If tokens are a scarce resource, that is a question of who gets them. A centre for AI and creativity should say so, and demonstrate the alternative: small, local, deliberate, made by people who mean it.

Pay per use, not per seat

One door

  • an institution-hosted gateway in front of commercial and open models: UiO GPT, GPT NTNU
  • chat, shared assistants, files, and now API keys for agents and coding tools
  • about 50 public-sector institutions run it

Keys by data class

  • one personal key per data class: red, yellow, green
  • the key is independent of the model
  • a monthly quota per model
  • a unit authorises a quota and pays for actual use

Good enough, not best

  • the best open-weight models, served on Fox and IDUN, cover most jobs
  • smaller models win on price per result
  • the frontier models on a quota when they are needed, without a seat per person
Depth. The short-term answer, from Oddmund Møgedal's presentation to the UiO idea meeting on 9 September. UiO built gpt.uio.no in 2023 as a privacy-safe chat front end where the providers do not know who the user is, and about 50 public-sector institutions now run it. Since 2025 it also serves open-weight models on Fox and on NTNU's IDUN, and the best of those are good enough for most of what people do. The new part is the API: personal keys, one per data class, so a red key only reaches models in the institution's own infrastructure, and the same key works from OpenCode, Claude Code, Claude Desktop, Codex or your own script. Quotas are limiting but necessary: they are what makes it possible to control the cost of the commercial models and to share the open ones. Claude and the more expensive OpenAI models through the API, with quotas and per-use billing, are in a pilot this month and planned for October, for the models available in Europe. The principle is what matters for the map: a unit pays for what its people actually use, across vendors, with as little lock-in as possible, and the person decides which model is good enough for the job. For MishMash the question is who does this for the partners with no gateway of their own: the art schools, the museums, the freelancers.

A proposal to argue with

How a centre could decide

A share, not a line

  • budget tokens as a share of personnel cost, as equipment always was
  • 3 to 5 per cent of a PhD year is 40 000 to 70 000 kroner
  • enough for heavy pay-per-use through a gateway

Quotas by role

  • a PhD in machine learning, a WP leader and a museum partner do not need the same budget
  • the gateway model, applied to the centre's own money
  • the unit sees what its people actually use

Not fungible

  • fellowships are in the contract; tokens come from operating money
  • so the real trade-off is tokens against a lab engineer, workshops, travel, hardware in the room
  • 50 seats is one engineer who builds the local alternative
Depth. Three rules a centre, or a department, could adopt this autumn. First, budget tokens as a percentage of personnel cost, the way equipment and computing have always been a few per cent on top of a position; at 3 to 5 per cent of a 1.4 million PhD year that is 40 000 to 70 000 kroner a year per person, which covers heavy pay-per-use through a gateway and is a decision the centre can make now. Second, quotas by role rather than seats by headcount, which is the gateway model from the previous slide applied to the centre's own money. Third, remember that the money is not fungible: fellowships are earmarked in the contract with the Research Council, and token spend comes from operating money, so the real trade-off is tokens against the other things operating money buys. A lab engineer costs about 1.5 million a year, which is 50 seats, and I would argue the engineer who builds the small, local alternative is the better buy. Sources: the Research Council's rates page for fellowships (1 372 000 kroner in 2025, actual costs from 2026) and Anthropic's published Max pricing (200 dollars a month).
